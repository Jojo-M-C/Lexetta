Walk through a forest and you might imagine that each tree stands alone, competing with its neighbours for light, water, and space. For a long time, that is exactly how scientists pictured woodlands: as a collection of individuals in silent competition. Over the past few decades, however, researchers have discovered something far stranger and more cooperative happening beneath our feet. Trees, it turns out, are connected to one another by a huge underground web — and the connectors are fungi.

Most people think of fungi only as mushrooms, but a mushroom is only the visible part of a much larger organism. The real body of the fungus is a mass of fine threads called hyphae, which spread through the soil in every direction. Together, these threads form a network known as mycelium, and a single teaspoon of forest soil can contain kilometres of it. When this fungal network wraps around and even enters the roots of a tree, it forms a partnership that biologists call a mycorrhiza — literally, a "fungus-root."

This partnership helps both sides. Trees are experts at capturing sunlight and turning it into sugar through photosynthesis, but they are not very good at pulling water and minerals out of the soil. Fungi have the opposite problem: they cannot make their own food, yet their thread-like bodies are perfectly shaped to take in nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus from a huge area of ground. So the two make a deal. The tree feeds the fungus with sugar; the fungus gives the tree water and nutrients it could never reach on its own. Neither partner plans this exchange, of course, but both depend on it to survive.

The truly surprising part is what happens next. Because a single fungal network can link the roots of many different trees, it allows resources to move not just between a tree and its fungus, but from one tree to another. In a series of famous experiments in Canadian forests, the ecologist Suzanne Simard showed that carbon could travel through these networks from one tree species to a completely different one. Older, larger trees — sometimes called "mother trees" — seemed to pass sugar to younger seedlings growing in the shade, helping them survive until they were tall enough to reach the light themselves.

Nutrients may not be the only thing that travels underground. Some studies suggest that when a tree is attacked by insects, chemical warning signals can pass through the fungal network to nearby trees, which then begin to prepare their own defences before the pests arrive. If this is true, it means the forest is not simply a crowd of separate organisms, but something closer to a connected community, quietly sharing food and information out of sight.

Discoveries like these have caught the public's interest, and the network even earned a catchy nickname: the "Wood Wide Web," a playful comparison to the internet that ties human society together. The image is attractive — a forest full of trees whispering to each other through a hidden cable of fungus — and it has changed the way many people think about nature.

It is worth being careful, however. Science moves forward by testing exciting ideas as strictly as possible, and in recent years some researchers have argued that the most dramatic claims have gone further than the evidence allows. It is clear that mycorrhizal networks exist and that fungi and trees exchange nutrients. What remains uncertain is how much trees really "help" one another through these connections in the wild, and whether talk of trees "communicating" gives them intentions they do not really have. Fungi, after all, may be acting in their own interest, moving resources in ways that suit themselves rather than deliberately looking after young trees.

None of this makes the discovery less impressive. If anything, it shows how much we still have to learn about a world that has been beneath our feet all along. The forest floor, once seen as little more than dirt and dead leaves, is now understood as one of the busiest and most complex places on the planet — a world where organisms from entirely different kingdoms of life have been trading, competing, and perhaps even cooperating for hundreds of millions of years. The next time you walk beneath the trees, it is worth remembering that the forest is not only above you. A lot of it, and much of its story, lies hidden in the ground below.
