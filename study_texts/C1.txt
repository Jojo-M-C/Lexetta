That time quickens as we grow older ranks among the most common of human intuitions, and among the hardest to explain. The child for whom a single summer seemed endless becomes, in time, the adult amazed that another year has already closed. The impression is intimate and almost universal, yet the moment we try to account for it, firm ground gives way beneath us.

The oldest explanation is really a matter of numbers. Each new year takes up a smaller share of the life already behind us: large when that life is brief, slight once it has grown long. What felt boundless at five, when it made up a fifth of everything, becomes a mere sliver at fifty, and the proportion alone seems enough to make the same interval feel ever smaller. Whether so simple a ratio can truly capture something as complex as lived experience is doubtful, but as a first idea it has genuine appeal. The calendar records no change, and yet the felt weight of a year quietly falls.

More persuasive, to many, is the argument from memory. The mind attends to whatever departs from expectation, and grows numb to whatever merely repeats. Novelty is therefore recorded in full and lasting detail, while repetition leaves scarcely a trace. The result is a curious difference: a stretch crowded with the unfamiliar feels wide and long in hindsight, densely filled with separate, vivid moments, whereas an equal stretch of routine, having kept almost nothing, contracts in memory to a brief and empty blur. We do not gauge the past by the hours it consumed, but by the trace it leaves behind, and routine leaves remarkably little.

The course of a life follows this logic with some precision. Early existence is filled with first encounters, each marked deeply because nothing preceded it. Maturity, by contrast, inclines toward settled habit and repetition, its days more and more like variations on themes already familiar. As the truly new grows scarce, so do the memories that once lent time its fullness, and one year begins to blur into the next. The weeks accumulate, yet very few remain distinct enough to be recalled.

Attention completes the account. The minutes that drag when we are restless, and the hours that dissolve when we are absorbed, reveal a basic difference between two orders of time: the constant, neutral time measured by the clock, and the flexible, shifting time we actually feel. The first never varies. The second expands and contracts without pause, and it is the second alone that we truly occupy. The same hour may feel vast or slight, according to nothing more than where the mind has settled.

A degree of caution is required here. However elegant these explanations may seem, none has secured anything close to agreement among those who study how we sense time. Each accounts for certain features of the experience while remaining silent about others, and the ways in which they interact remain poorly understood. The honest conclusion is that the feeling almost certainly arises from several causes at once, and that a full and settled explanation continues to elude us.

And yet the inquiry is not without practical value. If sameness is what thins our sense of time and hastens its passing, then the deliberate pursuit of novelty offers at least a partial remedy. To seek out the unfamiliar, to take on real difficulty, or simply to attend more closely to the ordinary texture of a given day is to furnish the memory more richly. None of this halts the calendar, whose progress remains indifferent to us. What it may do is subtler and perhaps more valuable: it can restore some depth to our days as we live them, so that a life, looked back upon, becomes something fuller than one long, quickening rush toward its close.
