To the casual observer, a forest presents itself as a gathering of solitary individuals, each tree straining upward in silent competition for light and moisture. For much of the twentieth century, ecologists shared this view, and thought of woodland as a place of constant rivalry. Yet beneath the leaf litter, an altogether different drama has been unfolding — one of exchange, dependency, and possibly even reciprocity — carried out not by the trees themselves but by the fungi wrapped around their roots.

The mushrooms we notice on the forest floor are only the reproductive structures of organisms whose true bulk lies hidden underground. The living body of a fungus consists of countless microscopic threads, or hyphae, which spread through the soil to form a sprawling web called a mycelium. Where these threads meet the roots of a plant, they may fuse with them to create a joined structure known as a mycorrhiza. This close association between fungus and root is not a rarity but the norm: the overwhelming majority of land plants host such partnerships, and forests could scarcely exist without them.

The logic of the arrangement is essentially economic. Through photosynthesis, trees produce sugars in abundance but struggle to extract scarce mineral nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, and the like — from the soil. Fungi, unable to photosynthesise, face the reverse problem, yet their fine threads spread through the ground so thoroughly that no root could match them, foraging for precisely those elements the tree lacks. The two therefore trade: carbon flows from tree to fungus, mineral nutrients and water in the opposite direction. It is a barter carried out without intention, refined over some four hundred million years of coevolution.

What has excited both scientists and the public is the discovery that a single mycelial network may connect many trees at once, turning a private exchange into a shared one. In pioneering fieldwork in the forests of British Columbia, the ecologist Suzanne Simard showed that carbon could travel through these underground channels between trees of different species, and that ancient "mother trees" appeared to support shaded seedlings struggling in the understorey. The implication was startling: a forest might work less as a collection of competitors than as a single, interdependent system.

The traffic underground may not be limited to food. A number of studies indicate that when a plant is attacked by aphids or other herbivores, chemical alarm signals can travel through the fungal network, prompting nearby plants to ready their own defences before an attack arrives. If this holds up, the forest would emerge as something close to a decentralised community, quietly passing around both resources and information beyond the reach of the naked eye.

Such findings gave rise to an irresistible metaphor: the "Wood Wide Web," a comparison to the digital network that connects human society. The image is appealing, conjuring up trees whispering to one another along threads of fungus, and it has strongly changed the way ordinary people see the natural world.

Some caution is needed, however. Science progresses by testing its most attractive ideas as strictly as possible, and a number of researchers have recently warned that the boldest claims have outrun the evidence. That mycorrhizal networks exist, and that trees and fungi trade nutrients, is beyond dispute. What remains contentious is the extent to which mature trees deliberately help their offspring in nature, and whether the language of "communication" and "cooperation" wrongly assigns purpose to organisms that possess none. The fungus, in particular, is no altruist; it may move resources in whatever way best serves its own growth, indifferent to the fate of any individual tree.

None of this diminishes the achievement. If anything, the debate shows how much remains unknown about a world that lies, quite literally, under our feet. The forest floor — long dismissed as dead soil and rotting leaves — is now recognised as among the most crowded and complex habitats on Earth, a place where organisms from completely different kingdoms of life have been bargaining, competing, and perhaps collaborating since long before the first human walked among the trees. To stroll through woodland, then, is to walk across the roof of a hidden city, most of whose business, and most of whose secrets, remain buried in the dark.
