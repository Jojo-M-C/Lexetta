Among the quiet discoveries that arrive with age, few unsettle us as deeply as the growing suspicion that time itself has begun to hurry. The summers that once seemed to open without limit, and the birthdays awaited across what felt like an unbridgeable distance, now give way with startling ease to years that close before we have fully registered their beginning. So ordinary is the feeling that we seldom pause upon it, and yet, held up to real scrutiny, it resists explanation with surprising stubbornness.

The most familiar account is also the most mechanical, and rests upon nothing more than proportion. Every year we live claims a smaller portion of the whole that precedes it. To the child of five, a single year amounts to a fifth of all remembered existence, a span of real magnitude, whereas to the person of fifty it is but one fraction among many, easily passed over. By this reckoning, the apparent quickening is little more than an illusion of arithmetic, each successive interval dwindling in weight as the sum of our years steadily mounts. Whether so neat a ratio can capture the lived texture of a life remains open to doubt, yet the intuition behind it is not easily dismissed.

A more searching explanation turns instead to the workings of memory. The mind fastens eagerly upon whatever is unfamiliar and grows quietly negligent toward whatever it has already met, so that novelty is laid down in abundant and enduring detail while routine leaves scarcely a mark. From this follows a curious inversion: a season thick with new experience, revisited in thought, appears richly populated and long, whereas a comparable season of routine, having deposited almost nothing, contracts in recollection to a brief and shapeless blur. We appraise the depth of our past not by the hours it consumed but by the residue those hours left in us, and mere repetition, however faithfully endured, leaves astonishingly little behind.

The trajectory of a human life conforms to this pattern with a certain melancholy precision. Our earliest years are crowded with first encounters, each engraved the more deeply for having no precedent whatever, while the years that follow incline, however agreeably, toward sameness and repetition. As the genuinely unprecedented grows ever scarcer, the memories that once lent time its fullness grow scarce as well, and one year folds all but indistinguishably into the next.

To complete the picture we must add the matter of attention. The minutes that stretch to an unbearable length while we wait, and the afternoons that dissolve unnoticed while we are wholly absorbed, disclose a quiet gulf between two orders of time: the even, impartial time recorded by any instrument, and the pliant, mood-dependent time that consciousness alone endures. The former is constant and utterly indifferent to us. The latter swells and contracts without cease, and it is within the latter alone that our lives are actually spent.

Honesty, however, demands restraint. However elegant these accounts may appear, not one has secured anything resembling consensus among those who study our perception of time. Each illuminates some corner of the mystery while consigning the rest to shadow, and how they might combine remains far from settled. The fairest conclusion is that the sensation almost certainly arises from several converging sources at once, and that a complete understanding still lies ahead of us.

The reflection nonetheless leaves us with something worth carrying forward. If it is sameness that hollows out our days and hastens them upon their way, then the deliberate pursuit of the unfamiliar, a willingness to be challenged, and above all a closer attention to the ordinary may restore to time some measure of the depth it seems to lose. Nothing we do will slow the turning of the calendar, which remains serenely untroubled by our wishes. Yet we may still learn to inhabit our days more fully, so that the life we look back upon may appear not as a single hurried passage toward its close, but as something wide, and varied, and unmistakably lived.
