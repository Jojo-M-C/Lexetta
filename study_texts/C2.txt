To the untutored eye, a forest resolves into a congregation of solitaries, each bole thrusting skyward in mute contention for photons and moisture. For the better part of a century, plant ecology proceeded upon much the same premise, construing the woodland as a Malthusian arena of unremitting competition. Beneath the humus, however, a subtler and more equivocal drama has long been transpiring — one of traffic, obligation, and perhaps reciprocity — orchestrated not by the trees but by the fungi enlaced about their rootlets.

The toadstools that stud the forest floor are but the ephemeral fruiting bodies of organisms whose substance resides, overwhelmingly, below ground. The vegetative fungus is an assemblage of hyphae, filaments of near-microscopic gauge that ramify through the substrate to constitute a mycelium of prodigious extent. Where these hyphae abut a root, they may enmesh or even penetrate it, forging the composite organ termed a mycorrhiza. Two principal guilds predominate: the ectomycorrhizal fungi, which sheathe the root in a dense mantle and characterise many temperate and boreal forest trees, and the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, which insinuate themselves within the very cells of most herbaceous plants. Such consortia are no aberration but the ancestral condition of terrestrial vegetation; the colonisation of dry land by plants was, in all likelihood, a fungal collaboration from its inception.

The rationale of the association is quintessentially mercantile. Trees, through the alchemy of photosynthesis, synthesise carbohydrate in surfeit yet prove maladroit at wresting the limiting mineral nutrients — chiefly nitrogen and phosphorus — from a recalcitrant soil. The fungi, debarred from photosynthesis, labour under the converse handicap, but their filiform bodies permeate the earth with an assiduity no root system could rival, scavenging precisely those elements in which their partner is deficient. Hence a barter: photosynthate is remitted to the fungus, mineral bounty and water reciprocated to the tree — a transaction devoid of deliberation yet honed across some four hundred million years of coevolution.

What has galvanised both the academy and the wider public is the recognition that an individual mycelium may anastomose the roots of many trees at once, converting a bilateral exchange into a putative commons. In seminal experiments amid the coniferous forests of British Columbia, the ecologist Suzanne Simard adduced evidence that carbon could transit these hypogeal conduits between phylogenetically distinct trees, and that senescent "mother trees" appeared to subsidise etiolated seedlings languishing in the understorey. The inference was arresting: the forest might cohere less as an aggregation of rivals than as a confederated, mutually indebted organism.

Nor need the subterranean commerce be confined to provender. Several investigations intimate that a plant beleaguered by aphids or analogous herbivores may broadcast infochemical alarms along the fungal skein, priming its neighbours to mobilise their secondary metabolites against imminent depredation. Were this to withstand replication, the woodland would approximate a decentralised polity, surreptitiously circulating both sustenance and intelligence beneath the threshold of perception.

From such findings sprang an irresistible trope: the "Wood Wide Web," an analogy to the reticulated architecture that binds human society. The conceit is beguiling — a stand of arboreal interlocutors susurrating along threads of fungus — and it has indelibly recast the vernacular understanding of nature.

Circumspection is nonetheless imperative. Science advances by consigning its most enticing conjectures to remorseless interrogation, and a growing cadre of researchers has latterly remonstrated that the most florid claims have outrun their evidentiary warrant. That mycorrhizal networks subsist, and that trees and fungi exchange nutrients, is incontrovertible. What abides in dispute is the degree to which mature trees purposively nurture their progeny under field conditions, and whether the idiom of "communication" and "altruism" illicitly attributes volition to entities bereft of it. The fungus above all is no philanthropist; it may reallocate resources in whatever fashion conduces to its own fecundity, sublimely indifferent to the destiny of any single tree.

None of this abates the wonder of the thing. If anything, the disputation lays bare how much remains inscrutable about a domain that lies, quite literally, underfoot. The forest floor — long disdained as inert tilth and rotting leaf-mould — now stands revealed as among the most teeming and labyrinthine biomes on the planet, a proscenium upon which organisms of radically divergent kingdoms have bargained, contended, and conceivably colluded since aeons before humankind first wandered beneath the canopy. To perambulate a wood, then, is to tread the roof of a buried metropolis, the bulk of whose transactions — and the whole of whose mysteries — persist, unlit, in the ground beneath.
